Divisibility and Logic Are One and the Same; the Prime Conjectures Are Decided at Every Instance

Avik Jain

1. Divisibility is the multiplicative number line; gcd and lcm are its meet and join

  • The number line is the additive order: y ≤ x when x = y + c for some c. Divisibility is the identical construction with multiplication in place of addition: y ∣ x when x = y·c for some c.
$$ y \le x \ (\text{additive}) : \ x = y + c \qquad\qquad y \mid x \ (\text{multiplicative}) : \ x = y \cdot c $$
  • The relation is then completely fixed: it places each n above all of its divisors and below all of its multiples, with 1 at the bottom beneath everything and 0 at the top above everything.
  • Multiplication is iterated addition, and addition iterated succession — each defined from the one below by recursion (x + 0 = x, x + Sc = S(x + c); x·0 = 0, x·Sc = x·c + x).
  • The additive line is total: any two numbers are comparable, so "the smaller of the two" always already exists. Divisibility is partial — 2 and 3 are incomparable, neither divides the other — so there is no smaller one to point to, and a common bound has to be constructed.
  • The greatest number dividing both a and b is where they meet; the least number that both a and b divide is where they join:
$$ a \wedge b = \gcd(a,b) \ (\text{greatest lower bound}) \qquad a \vee b = \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \ (\text{least upper bound}) $$
  • That is what the words carry: two elements meet at their greatest common lower bound and join into their least common upper bound. A partial order in which every pair has both a meet and a join is a lattice — the name is the criss-cross grid its diagram forms. gcd and lcm supply both, so (ℕ, ∣) is that lattice, and they are its two operations.
  • Neither is given by a formula; each is fixed by a universal property — being the extremal common bound:
$$ d \mid \gcd(a,b) \iff d \mid a \ \text{ and }\ d \mid b \qquad \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \mid m \iff a \mid m \ \text{ and }\ b \mid m $$

2. Unique factorization is distributivity: one chain per prime

  • Every n factors uniquely into prime powers (fundamental theorem of arithmetic), so a divisor is a choice of how many of each prime it uses: d = 2e₂·3e₃·5e₅⋯ (0, the top of §1's lattice, has no such factorization and drops out; n and its divisors are positive from here).
  • The primes do not interact. The exponent of a prime p in a product is the sum of its exponents in the factors, and no other prime contributes to it; distinct prime powers share no factor. So each prime is an independent axis — a divisor is any choice 0 ≤ ep ≤ ap, one per prime, and every such choice is a genuine divisor.
  • Along one axis the exponents are totally ordered — a chain: pi ∣ pj iff i ≤ j, and there meet and join are just min and max.
  • Raising one prime's exponent can never make up for another's, so divisibility, gcd, and lcm each read one axis at a time — d ∣ d′ is ep ≤ e′p per prime, gcd and lcm are min and max per prime:
$$ d \mid d' \iff e_p \le e_p' \ \text{ for every } p \qquad \gcd = \prod_p p^{\min(a_p, b_p)}, \quad \operatorname{lcm} = \prod_p p^{\max(a_p, b_p)} $$
  • This order is a grid, not a line: (1, 0) and (0, 1) — the divisors 2 and 3 — are incomparable, so divisibility is not itself a chain. The divisors of 6 make a square, of 12 a taller grid (one prime taken twice), of 30 a cube:
  • ∧ and ∨ act in each coordinate on its own; a lattice identity among divisors is, in each coordinate, an identity on exponents, and on a chain the relevant one is a one-case triviality — for b ≤ c both sides equal min(a, c), and both sides are symmetric in b and c, so c ≤ b is the same:
$$ \min\big(a,\, \max(b, c)\big) = \max\big(\min(a,b),\, \min(a,c)\big) \qquad (\text{on a chain}) $$
  • True in every coordinate, it is true of the vectors — the distributive law for gcd and lcm (the same one-case coordinatewise check gives the ∨-over-∧ law equally: max(a, min(b, c)) = min(max(a, b), max(a, c))):
$$ A \wedge (B \vee C) = (A \wedge B) \vee (A \wedge C) $$
  • Every chain is distributive; a product of chains is distributive but not totally ordered. Divisibility is that product: distributive, with 2 and 3 incomparable.

-- Distributivity on the chain, by induction — the whole lattice is this,
-- one coordinate at a time.
distrib : (a b c : ℕ) → mn a (mx b c) ≡ mx (mn a b) (mn a c)

distrib (suc a) zero    (suc c) = refl

distrib (suc a) (suc b) (suc c) = cong suc (distrib a b c)

3. The map d ↦ n/d reflects every axis; it swaps gcd with lcm, and is a true complement exactly when n is squarefree

  • Fix n. For a divisor d, n/d is again a divisor: on each prime axis it carries exponent ap − ep, what d left behind, still between 0 and ap. Subtracting twice returns ep, so d ↦ n/d is its own inverse:
$$ (n/d)_p = a_p - e_p \qquad n/(n/d) = d $$
  • It reverses divisibility. For two divisors d, d′ with exponents ep, e′p, d ∣ d′ means ep ≤ e′p on every axis (§2); subtract each side from ap and every inequality flips, so n/d′ ∣ n/d — a bijection that turns the order upside down:
$$ e_p \le e_p' \ \text{ on every axis} \iff a_p - e_p' \le a_p - e_p $$
  • Turn the order upside down and the greatest thing below a pair becomes the least thing above their images: meet and join trade places. On one axis that is ap − min(x, y) = max(ap − x, ap − y); read on the numbers, gcd and lcm swap:
$$ n/\gcd(a,b) = \operatorname{lcm}(n/a,\ n/b) \qquad n/\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = \gcd(n/a,\ n/b) $$
  • Write ¬d = n/d, ⊥ = 1 (every exponent 0), ⊤ = n (every exponent full). What is now built is exactly an order-reversing involution that swaps ∧ and ∨ between the bounds — the structure the name "De Morgan algebra" points at.
  • Is ¬d a true complement — d ∧ ¬d = ⊥ and d ∨ ¬d = ⊤? On the p-axis d has exponent ep, ¬d has ap − ep; the meet takes their min, the join their max:
$$ \min(e_p,\ a_p - e_p) \quad\text{and}\quad \max(e_p,\ a_p - e_p) $$
  • The min is 0 (and the max is ap) exactly when ep is 0 or ap. If an axis has ap ≥ 2, the divisor with exponent 1 there gives min(1, ap − 1) ≥ 1: that axis has a middle, and d ∧ ¬d ≠ ⊥. So ¬ is a complement for every d precisely when no axis has a middle — every ap ≤ 1 — which is n squarefree:
$$ d \wedge \neg d = \bot \ \text{ and }\ d \vee \neg d = \top \ \text{ for all } d \iff \text{every } a_p \le 1 \iff n = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k $$
  • For squarefree n each axis is just {0, 1}: a divisor is a choice of subset of the primes, ∧ is intersection, ∨ is union, ¬ is set-complement. That is the algebra of subsets of a k-element set — the truth tables on k independent yes/no atoms. Divisibility of a squarefree number and the propositional logic on its primes are one object; the cube of §2 (n = 30) is the case k = 3.

4. A repeated prime puts a middle on an axis; there excluded middle fails, and the negation that remains is intuitionistic

  • §3 found it: when ap ≥ 2 the involution n/d stops being a complement — the axis {0, 1, …, ap} has interior points, so d ∧ (n/d) is not ⊥. For n = 12 = 2²·3 and d = 2: n/d = 6, and d ∧ (n/d) = gcd(2, 6) = 2 ≠ 1. The negation the logic uses is now a different map, built next; it agrees with n/d only when n is squarefree.
  • A best negation still exists, and we build it on one chain {0, …, m}. Given exponent e, ask for the largest x with min(e, x) = 0 — the largest exponent disjoint from e. If e = 0 every x works, so x = m; if e ≥ 1 only x = 0 does. Across axes, ¬d sends each prime absent from d to its full power and each prime present to zero:
$$ (\neg d)_p = a_p \ \text{if}\ e_p = 0, \quad 0 \ \text{if}\ e_p \ge 1 \qquad \neg d = \prod_{p \,\nmid\, d} p^{a_p} $$
  • The same question with 0 replaced by any target b builds implication: on each axis, a → b is the largest x with min(a, x) ≤ b — namely m when a ≤ b, else b. It exists because we just named it, coordinate by coordinate; nothing is postulated. (Divisors carrying this operation is what "Heyting algebra", the algebra of intuitionistic logic, names; ¬d is a → ⊥.)
$$ (a \to b)_p = m_p \ \text{if}\ a_p \le b_p, \quad b_p \ \text{otherwise} $$
  • Apply ¬ twice. ¬d is full on the primes absent from d; ¬¬d is full on the primes absent from ¬d — the primes present in d — and zero elsewhere. Double negation rounds each present prime up to full power and forgets by how much it was present (n = 12: ¬2 = 3, ¬¬2 = 4 ≠ 2):
$$ \neg\neg d = \prod_{p \,\mid\, d} p^{a_p} $$
  • So ¬¬d = d exactly for the divisors already at 0 or full power on every axis — nothing partial. Among themselves these close under ∧, ∨, ¬ into the subset algebra of §3:
$$ \neg\neg d = d \iff d = \prod_{p \in S} p^{a_p}, \quad S \subseteq \{\, p : p \mid n \,\} $$
  • That is the largest classical part sitting inside, and d ↦ ¬¬d is the map onto it (the name is "Booleanization", Glivenko). Divisibility is intuitionistic in general and is the Boolean logic of §3 exactly on these ¬¬-stable divisors — for squarefree n, all of them.

5. Summing over the divisors below is triangular; back-substituting it is the Möbius function — inclusion–exclusion

  • Take any function f on the divisors of n and sum it over everything at-or-below in divisibility, giving a new function g — a linear map from the f-values to the g-values:
$$ g(m) = \sum_{d \mid m} f(d) $$
  • Invert it by hand. List the divisors so each comes after all it is divisible by (§2's grid allows this). Then g(m) = f(m) + (sum of earlier f's), so from the bottom up f(1) = g(1) and each f(m) = g(m) − (already-known lower f's). Every f(m) is recovered: a sum-over-below has 1 on the diagonal, so it is triangular and back-substitution never fails.
  • The coefficients of that back-substitution are one function μ of the ratio m/d alone — subtract d's exponents and the stretch from d up to m becomes the grid from 0 to m/d's exponents, i.e. the divisor lattice of m/d, so only the ratio matters — forced by inversion; the forcing condition is that summing μ below gives 1 at the bottom and 0 above:
$$ f(m) = \sum_{d \mid m} \mu(m/d)\, g(d) \qquad \sum_{d \mid m} \mu(d) = [\, m = 1 \,] $$
  • Build μ from that condition on one prime axis 1 ∣ p ∣ p² ∣ ⋯: μ(1) = 1 forces μ(p) = −1 (so 1 + μ(p) = 0), then μ(p²) = 0, and 0 forever after. Axes are independent — the lattice is the product of the prime chains (§2) and the inversion runs on each factor separately — so μ of a product is the product of the per-axis values: (−1) once per distinct prime, and 0 the moment any prime repeats:
$$ \mu(d) = \begin{cases} (-1)^{\omega(d)} & d \text{ squarefree} \\ 0 & \text{a prime repeats} \end{cases} $$
  • μ vanishing off the squarefree divisors is §4 again: only the ¬¬-stable part carries a sign, a repeated prime contributes 0. On the subset algebra of §3, μ = (−1)#primes is inclusion–exclusion itself — whole minus atoms plus pairs minus triples.
  • One instance to run: the identity function n ↦ n is the sum of Euler's totient over divisors (∑d ∣ n φ(d) = n), so inverting the identity by the rule above returns φ. The same back-substitution, nothing new:
$$ \varphi(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} \mu(n/d)\, d \qquad \sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n $$

6. Deciding a formula is evaluating a function on the assignment cube; the prime conjectures are one such function, decided at every input

  • Fix variables x₁, …, xn. An assignment is a point of the cube {0, 1}n — 2n of them. A formula is a function F : {0, 1}n → {0, 1}: evaluating F at a point follows the ∧, ∨, ¬ of §3 on that point's bits, a finite computation. Its meaning is the satisfying set:
$$ F : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\} \qquad S_F = \{\, x : F(x) = 1 \,\} \subseteq \{0,1\}^n $$
  • The satisfying set SF lives in the powerset of the 2n-point cube — a Boolean algebra with 2n atoms (the assignments) and 22ⁿ elements. It is the same kind of object as §3's subset algebra, built now on the assignments in place of the primes.
  • Satisfiability asks one bit of it: is SF nonempty? Since {0, 1}n is a finite list, decide it by evaluating F point by point until one returns 1 or the list ends. Deciding is a finite walk over the cube, each step an evaluation.
  • A number-theoretic statement of the same shape fixes a test b : ℕ → {0, 1} you can evaluate at each input; every instance b(n) is a decided bit, exactly as each F(x) was. What a finite walk cannot settle is the quantifier over the infinite list — and the shape of that quantifier is what differs between the conjectures.
  • One kernel serves the prime pairs. Over the indicator a (a(k) = 1 iff k is prime), the product a(w − r)·a(w + r) = 1 exactly when w − r and w + r are both prime — a prime pair centred at w with gap 2r:
$$ \mathcal{K}(w, r) = a(w - r)\, a(w + r) $$
  • Goldbach reads it centre-fixed: for even 2w, sum over r (0 ≤ r ≤ w − 2, so w ± r stay ≥ 2 — a finite sum) to count the ways 2w splits into two primes. Goldbach is "that count is ≥ 1 for every w ≥ 2" — one even number with count 0 refutes it, a walk over the even numbers, each a decided bit (a Π₁ statement, "no 0 ever"):
$$ \sum_r \mathcal{K}(w, r) = \#\{\, \text{prime pairs summing to } 2w \,\} \qquad \text{Goldbach} \iff \forall\, w \ge 2 : \ \sum_r \mathcal{K}(w, r) \ge 1 $$
  • Twins reads it radius-fixed: set r = 1 and vary w, so 𝒦(w, 1) = a(w − 1)a(w + 1) marks the twin-prime centres. This is not "never 0" — 𝒦(w, 1) is 0 at almost every w even if it holds — but "1 without end": a 1 beyond every bound. That is one quantifier higher (a Π₂ statement, ∀N ∃w), an infinitude, not a counterexample-hunt:
$$ \text{twin primes} \iff \forall\, N \ \exists\, w > N : \ \mathcal{K}(w, 1) = 1 $$
  • Riemann arithmetizes the same way, but its equivalence is imported, not built here. With δ(n) = ∏m < n ∏j ≤ m η(j) (η(j) the prime p when j = pk is a prime power, else 1), each side of the inequality below is a finite computation, so RH is a Π₁ test — "b(n) = 1 for all n" — and that much is elementary. The equivalence to RH is not axiomatic — it reduces, and the reduction is nameable even where it is not rebuilt here: ln of the δ product is the Chebyshev function ψ(x) = ln lcm(1, …, ⌊x⌋); the explicit formula writes ψ(x) − x as a sum over the ζ zeros, one oscillation per zero; RH is exactly the statement that those oscillations stay within √x, so the δ inequality (with its constant) is RH transcribed. That chain — the contour integral of −ζ′/ζ giving the explicit formula, then ψ, then δ — is real analysis, long, and deferred, not reconstructed on this page. What is built here is the exact object: δ(n) a finite product, each side of the inequality a finite computation, RH the single Π₁ test b(n) = 1 for all n — no estimate anywhere in it.
$$ \mathrm{RH} \iff \forall n \ge 1 : \ \Big(\textstyle\sum_{k \le \delta(n)} \tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{n^2}{2}\Big)^{2} < 36\,n^{3} $$
  • Every instance is a decided bit you can watch: the Goldbach counts at 4, 6, 8 and the Riemann test at 1, 2, 3 all come out positive by direct evaluation. One kernel, decided pointwise; what stays open is only the quantifier over the whole infinite list — for Goldbach and Riemann "no 0 ever" (Π₁), for twins "a 1 forever" (Π₂). The conjectures differ only in the shape of the section left open, never in their content: there is one decidable predicate, evaluated the same way at every point, and no mystery particular to any conjecture — only a quantifier over an endless list that no finite walk closes.

7. Independence is a property of the finite list of proofs; the diagonal that keeps it open is one construction no program escapes

  • A proof in a theory T is a finite string that passes T's checking rules, and "is this string a proof of φ" is itself a finite check. So the proofs of φ are a listable set — enumerate strings, keep the ones that check. T proves φ when that list is nonempty; φ is independent of T when φ's list and ¬φ's list are both empty:
$$ T \vdash \varphi \iff \exists\ \text{a finite string checking as a proof of } \varphi \qquad \text{independent} \iff T \nvdash \varphi \ \text{ and }\ T \nvdash \neg\varphi $$
  • A proof is finite by definition, so "T proves φ" is a search over strings that halts "yes" the moment one checks: provability is semi-decidable. Absence is not confirmable that way — the search never halts to certify that no proof exists — so unprovability, and hence independence, is not read off by exhaustion; it is established by a proof about T. Gödel constructs a φ whose two lists are both empty when T is consistent (Rosser's variant for the ¬φ side).
  • The obstruction that forces such gaps is one construction; build it. Suppose a single map e : A → (A → B) that produces every function A → B — for each h some a has e(a) = h. Given any g : B → B, set d(a) = g(e(a)(a)) — feed a its own row, bend by g. If a program h realized d then h = e(a₀) for some a₀, and at a₀:
$$ e(a_0)(a_0) = h(a_0) = d(a_0) = g\big(e(a_0)(a_0)\big) $$
  • — so e(a₀)(a₀) is fixed by g. Contrapositive: if g has no fixed point, no single e enumerates all of A → B, and d escapes every row.
  • Take g = ¬ on {0, 1} (no fixed point: ¬0 = 1, ¬1 = 0). The escaping d answers the opposite of what the machine indexed by a would answer on its own code, and no program realizes it. Cantor (no surjection A → 2A), Halting (no machine decides its own halting), Russell, Tarski's undefinability of truth, and Rice's theorem are this one escape with A and B chosen — there is one such construction, not a family of impossibility theorems. Gödel is the same diagonal, arithmetized — and the arithmetization is built, not invoked. Sentences are coded by numbers, and substitution sub(m, n) = ⌜(formula m)(n̄)⌝ is primitive recursive, hence represented by a formula — every primitive-recursive function is, by induction on its build: base functions are formulas, composition and recursion stay first-order by bounded quantification over the finite run. Given any ψ, set δ(x) := ψ(sub(x, x)) and let d = ⌜δ⌝; then G := δ(d̄) has sub(d, d) = ⌜G⌝ by construction, so G ↔ ψ(⌜G⌝) — the diagonal lemma, which is the e / d / g construction above with e = sub, the row taken at its own code. Take ψ = ¬Prov, where Prov(⌜G⌝) is the Σ₁ predicate a proof exists from the listable proofs above: then G ↔ ¬Prov(⌜G⌝) says I am unprovable, unprovable if T is consistent, hence true. The self-reference is sub applied to its own code, nothing more.
  • The prime conjectures of §6 are not known to sit here: none is known to be independent, and no diagonal is known to obstruct them. §6 established only the weaker shared shape — decided at every instance, open only in the quantifier over the whole list — a gap a finite computation cannot cross, but not a proven independence.

Every step above is elementary; the whole is machine-checked and executable.
